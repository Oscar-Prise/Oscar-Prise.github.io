I

Feasibility — Commercial Teardown & Motor Conversion

Our PI wanted to benchmark a commercial device against the lab's own past-student designs. I disassembled the commercial unit and converted it to accept our motor and Jetson, which used a completely different input/output pattern than the stock hardware — and the PI didn't want the commercial unit damaged, so this had to be non-destructive reverse engineering with no CAD or engineering drawings to start from.

The hardest part was mounting the motor driver securely. Without a way to see the true hole pattern on the commercial housing, earlier mounting attempts left the encoder reader misaligned. I ended up projecting the part to a larger scale with a shadow-casting rig, then measured the hole coordinates on a lathe to recover the pattern precisely enough to design a proper adaptor — about two weeks of work.

Along the way, the PLA I was printing with deformed and melted from motor heat, which sent me back to respec the printer materials before continuing.

II

First Validation

Metabolic testing on the converted device showed a 1% reduction versus no exoskeleton — a single subject, but the first measurable metabolic benefit this lab had produced. The past student's device hadn't shown one. That result was enough for our PI to fully commit to a ground-up reverse-engineering of the device.

III

Full Reverse-Engineered Device

I reverse-engineered the complete commercial device — my CAD ended up visually near-identical to the original, built entirely from measurement and observation since no manufacturer drawings existed to start from. Our 3D printer was too small for the full-scale parts, so I redesigned around that constraint while preserving the structure, keeping everything manufacturable in-house.

Hinge failures

The abduction/adduction hinge was too weak for the loads passing through it. I tried several mechanical hinge designs; all of them broke under load, which pushed a custom hinge onto the roadmap for a future iteration.

The pelvis interface

At high torque, the motor rocked forward and backward. My first hypothesis was the pelvis interface — I iterated through several rough geometries, testing fit directly on people to see where the gaps were. Eventually I took a cue from Georgia Tech's device design: using surfacing to generate a generic hip profile, then building the pelvis interface as an offset of that profile. Fit improved significantly, though rocking itself wasn't solved yet — that's Generation Two, next.

IV

The Motor Rocking Problem

Even with the improved pelvis interface from Generation One, the device still rocked forward and backward under high torque. The cause turned out to be the thigh interface, not the pelvis. Redesigning it into a calf-like structure eliminated the rocking entirely, up to 17 N·m — a clear before/after, not just a marginal improvement. This is the strongest documentation in the whole project: two side-by-side videos showing the calf design against the old one under identical load.

V

The Weight Trade-off

Solving the rocking issue brought the weight back up from 3.1kg to around 4kg — which on paper works against the point of a lightweight design. I'm treating that honestly as a trade-off rather than a regression: a fully weight-optimized structure couldn't survive the loads it needed to, so the extra mass buys the structural integrity the device actually needs to function.

VI

Generalizing With a PhD Collaborator

Working with a PhD student in the lab, the interface was generalized past a single fitted design: the pelvis interface now fits a range of body sizes, and a straight-bar version was added as an alternate configuration. A pilot metabolic test across three subjects showed a meaningful reduction in effort for all three — early-stage pilot testing, not a completed study, so I'm stating that qualitatively rather than citing exact numbers here.
