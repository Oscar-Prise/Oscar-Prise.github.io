Record I — Origin

How I Got Here

I grew up on Warhammer and Halo, which is probably the honest answer for why exoskeletons pulled at me before I understood what the field actually was.

In undergrad at Virginia Tech, I was doing fairly traditional mechanical engineering coursework, and I hadn't found my direction yet. I applied to a robotics lab and got rejected — I simply wasn't competitive enough at the time. That rejection turned out to be the reason I'm here now. It was the wake-up call I needed to actually commit to the field instead of drifting through it.

After graduating, I took a couple of jobs that didn't line up with what I wanted (see my service record), and realized how much I was missing to actually build an exoskeleton myself. At the time, I thought the gap was hardware, electronics, and control — full stop. So I took an embedded systems research position specifically to build that mechatronics foundation before going further.

I came to Carnegie Mellon to study control and close what I thought was the last gap. I ended up in the Metamobility Lab, and getting close to real exoskeleton hardware showed me the field is further from what I'd imagined than I expected — and that my three-part list was incomplete.

Exoskeleton control isn't automotive or humanoid control. It's not really about PID or nonlinear control theory the way I'd assumed — it's about mapping human intent into assistance, in real time, for a person who's wearing the result. And underneath all of it, you need to understand human biomechanics, not just hardware and software.

Hardware and control carry equal weight. Great hardware with bad control still feels inhuman to move in. Great control on the wrong hardware never reaches anyone.

That's the balance I'm building toward now — roughly 60% hardware, 40% control, combined.

I

Feasibility — Commercial Teardown & Motor Conversion

Our PI wanted to benchmark a commercial device against the lab's own past-student designs. I disassembled the commercial unit and converted it to accept our motor and Jetson, which used a completely different input/output pattern than the stock hardware — and the PI didn't want the commercial unit damaged, so this had to be non-destructive reverse engineering with no CAD or engineering drawings to start from.

The hardest part was mounting the motor driver securely. Without a way to see the true hole pattern on the commercial housing, earlier mounting attempts left the encoder reader misaligned. I ended up projecting the part to a larger scale with a shadow-casting rig, then measured the hole coordinates on a lathe to recover the pattern precisely enough to design a proper adaptor — about two weeks of work.

Along the way, the PLA I was printing with deformed and melted from motor heat, which sent me back to respec the printer materials before continuing.

II

First Validation

Metabolic testing on the converted device showed a 1% reduction versus no exoskeleton — a single subject, but the first measurable metabolic benefit this lab had produced. The past student's device hadn't shown one. That result was enough for our PI to fully commit to a ground-up reverse-engineering of the device.

III

Full Reverse-Engineered Device

I reverse-engineered the complete commercial device — my CAD ended up visually near-identical to the original. Our 3D printer was too small for the full-scale parts, so I redesigned around that constraint while preserving the structure, keeping everything manufacturable in-house.

Hinge failures

The abduction/adduction hinge was too weak for the loads passing through it. I tried several mechanical hinge designs; all of them broke under load, which pushed a custom hinge onto the roadmap for a future iteration.

The motor rocking problem

At high torque, the motor rocked forward and backward. My first hypothesis was the pelvis interface — I iterated through several rough geometries, testing fit directly on people to see where the gaps were. Eventually I took a cue from Georgia Tech's device design: using surfacing to generate a generic hip profile, then building the pelvis interface as an offset of that profile. Fit improved significantly.

Rocking persisted even with the pelvis fixed, which pointed to the thigh interface instead. Redesigning it into a calf-like structure eliminated rocking up to 17 N·m — a clear before/after, not just a marginal improvement.

IV

Trade-off & Generalization

Solving the rocking issue brought the weight back up from 3.1kg to around 4kg — which on paper works against the point of a lightweight design. I'm treating that honestly as a trade-off rather than a regression: a fully weight-optimized structure couldn't survive the loads it needed to, so the extra mass buys the structural integrity the device actually needs to function.

From there, I worked with a PhD student to generalize the interface — a pelvis that fits multiple body sizes, alongside a straight-bar design — and ran a pilot metabolic test across three subjects, with a positive reduction across all of them.
